Overview

Credora is a curation layer, not a lending protocol. Markets, interest, collateral and liquidations are handled by the underlying lending protocol. Credora's role is to choose which markets exist, the oracle behind each, the caps a vault lends under, and the machinery intended to keep bad positions from becoming bad debt.

Two contracts of the protocol may touch money and neither should hold it between transactions: a liquidator that works on a flash loan, and a router that opens a leveraged position in a single transaction.

Core principle. Understand what is live, what is planned, and what can go wrong. A market's parameters and its available liquidity are not interchangeable.

Getting started

Five steps make up the basic flow. The third is the only one that costs anything to get wrong.

  1. Connect a wallet. Connecting should read balances only; it must not move tokens without a signature from you.
  2. Post collateral. On a market page, set the LTV slider to zero and the button becomes Post collateral. Collateral does not touch the lending pool, so you can take your place before there is anything to borrow, owe nothing while you wait, and withdraw again whenever allowed.
  3. Borrow. The slider should show the health factor and liquidation price before you sign, and refuse to pass the safe cap by default.
  4. Watch the health factor. Below 1.0 the position may be liquidated by anyone. The liquidation price is often more useful than today's price.
  5. Repay and withdraw. Repayment may be tracked by shares rather than a quoted asset amount because debt accrues every second. A quoted amount can be short in the next block and leave dust that keeps collateral locked.

Review every transaction and its parameters before signing.

How a market gets listed

A market is defined by four immutable choices — collateral, loan asset, oracle and LLTV — plus a cap the vault sets and can change. Getting the first four wrong cannot be fixed by editing the market; a new market would be needed.

Prove the token address

A ticker symbol does not prove token identity. A popular ticker may have many tokens associated with it, including impostors. Token addresses should be checked against the issuer and the appropriate on-chain evidence.

Choose the price source

Use a Chainlink feed where one exists; otherwise a Uniswap v3 arithmetic-mean-tick TWAP may be used over the token's USDG pool. A TWAP market should not be created until the pool's observation ring is long enough for the intended window.

Set the LLTV tier

LLTV is a tier, not a judgement call. Example tiers in the reference are 86.0% for treasuries, 77.0% for ETH, 62.5% for ordinary stocks and 38.5% for long-tail markets. The lending protocol permits only a fixed set of values, so conservative rounding may be required.

Size the cap from exit depth

A cap should reflect measured exit liquidity, not ambition. If the pool cannot absorb a liquidation sale without moving the price beyond the liquidation premium, the cap is too high.

At 50% utilisation, a market pays its lenders roughly half what its borrowers pay because half the pool is idle.

Vaults

A vault is an ERC-4626 that spreads one asset across several markets under caps. You deposit USDG and receive shares; the shares appreciate as yield accrues. You are not choosing markets individually — the curator is, and caps are the limits of that decision.

A cap can only be raised slowly. Raising one is submitted, then waits out the vault's timelock before it can be accepted. Lowering a cap is immediate because the safe direction should never be the slow one.

A guardian can veto during that window. A vault cannot invent a market; it can only allocate into markets that already exist, each with its oracle, LLTV and cap.

The reference describes a performance fee of 10% of interest earned, waived for the first 90 days. It is charged on yield, never on principal. Verify the current configuration before relying on these figures.

Withdrawals

A deposit can be withdrawn as long as it is not lent out. When a market is fully used, the rate climbs, which can pull in supply and push borrowers to repay — but until that happens, part of a deposit may genuinely be unavailable.

Every vault card should show withdrawable now next to total assets. Withdrawable is the number to trust for immediate liquidity; total assets describe what you own, not necessarily what you can take out this minute.

Risk framework

Each market carries a liquidation LTV fixed at creation. Example tiers in the reference are 86% for T-bills, 77% for ETH, 62.5% for megacaps and index ETFs, and 38.5% for long-tail assets or assets priced by TWAP.

The dangerous moment for a stock market is often Monday's open, not the weekend itself. Nothing trades while a feed is still, so the LTV has to survive the gap when it moves again. Caps should only rise against measured exit depth.

Liquidation LTV

The threshold at which a position becomes eligible for liquidation. It is fixed at market creation.

Supply cap

A limit on exposure, sized around the liquidity that could be exited in a stressed market.

Oracles

Stocks and ETFs may be priced by Chainlink through the lending protocol's oracle factory. Those feeds run 24/5 and can hold their last print from Friday close to Monday open. That pause can be normal rather than a fault.

Long-tail tickers without a feed may be priced by a Uniswap v3 TWAP over a 30–60 minute window on a pool whose observation cardinality has been raised first. Where both sources exist, a conservative design may take the lower price source.

Observation cardinality matters. If a pool stores fewer past price points than the TWAP window needs, the oracle may stop answering. Raise the observation ring before relying on a longer window.

Short markets

A short market inverts the usual pair: you post USDG as collateral and borrow the stock itself, then sell it. If the stock falls you buy it back cheaper and keep the difference. The debt is denominated in shares, so it grows when the stock rises — the exposure a short is supposed to have.

The oracle is the long market's oracle turned upside down: one over the price, on the same scale the lending protocol expects. Shorts can run at a conservative LTV when the requested tier is not permitted. The practical limit is often the borrow side: you can only short what somebody has supplied.

Liquidations

Above the liquidation LTV, anyone may repay part of a debt and seize collateral at a bonus set by the lending protocol's formula. A keeper can use a flash loan and sell collateral through a route that has been simulated.

Pre-liquidation

Before full liquidation, a borrower may be able to authorise a partial unwind between two thresholds at a smaller penalty. This can turn a price gap into a trimmed position rather than a closed one, if the relevant contracts and market support it.

Independent liquidators

A liquidation that would revert should never leave the machine. A sale below the oracle floor should be refused by the contract. A protocol-run liquidator should not be the only liquidator: if it is offline or out of gas, other liquidators should still be able to act. An indexer should not be the only source of truth for critical on-chain decisions.

Multiply

Leverage in one transaction can be built by flash-loaning the loan asset, swapping it into collateral, supplying that collateral, borrowing against it and repaying the flash loan. The health factor and liquidation price should be computed before signing, and the swap should revert if the pool has moved far enough from the oracle that the position would open at a bad price.

Credit scores

Behaviour can be scored off chain from information an indexer sees: how long positions stay healthy, whether they are topped up before trouble and how often they are liquidated. A good score may buy a better rebate — never a higher LTV, because the LTV protects lenders.

Points

A points season can count size multiplied by time: a hundred dollars supplied for ten days and a thousand for one day are worth the same. Points may accrue while a user does nothing and be counted off chain.

There is no promised conversion rate. Naming a price before there are earnings to pay it would make a promise out of money the protocol does not yet have.

The launchpad

A coin launched through the reference launchpad is minted by Pons, not by Cluby. Pons owns the token, the bonding curve and the tax; the launchpad registers the coin and directs tax to a protocol-controlled recipient.

The launcher picks a tax rate up to Pons's 10% cap, and Pons charges its own 1% pair fee on top. The tax rate is fixed at launch and cannot be changed afterwards, including by the protocol.

How the tax divides

The tax divides four ways: the largest share is supplied into one Cluby market as a lender and left there; another share buys the coin back on its own curve and burns it; another goes to the launcher; and the remainder funds the protocol. Exact shares should be read from the deployed launchpad contract, not duplicated as independent values across pages.

The lending principal is locked

The lent share is a one-way door. The withdrawal path should only allow what the position earned above principal to leave. No owner function should reach the principal. What the position earns can be spent buying the coin, and purchased coins are destroyed based on the balance actually held afterwards.

Why buyback is separate

Splitting can be permissionless so the treasury does not wait on a keeper. A bonding-curve trade without a slippage bound could be exposed to sandwiching. The burn share can accrue instead, and a keeper spends it under a bound it computes.

What can go wrong

  • A coin is not the stock it is named after. It owns no shares and tracks no price. It lends into one market, and what it earns is what that market's borrowers pay — nothing, if nobody borrows.
  • The curve can be sold into. Pons owns the price. Burning shrinks the supply; it does not put a floor under it.
  • A supply position can sit idle. A quiet market is a coin that stops burning.
  • A coin is not itself a lending market. Launching one does not make it collateral; a market against it is a separate decision based on measured depth.
Availability note. The reference documentation describes launchpad mechanics, while the issuance interface may still be marked as coming soon. This page documents the flow; it does not claim that a real token can be launched from this frontend alone.

Builders

An app that routes volume can append its address as twenty bytes at the end of a call. Solidity ignores bytes past the arguments it expects, so it costs calldata gas and changes nothing about how the transaction runs — no extra contract needs to sit in the path of users' deposits to collect a statistic.

A suffix is a claim, not proof: anyone can append any address to their own transaction. What is paid should be settled against the registered list.

Flash loans

Morpho can lend an asset it holds for the length of one transaction at zero fee, subject to the deployed contract's actual behaviour and current network. Call flashLoan(token, assets, data) on the relevant contract and repay inside the callback.

Always verify contract addresses against current deployment records before sending a transaction. Example addresses in documentation are not a substitute for on-chain verification.

Conventions

Money is a number in whole units, already scaled — totalSupplyUsd: 1250.5 means $1,250.50, not wei. Rates and ratios are fractions, so 0.0625 is 6.25%. Addresses are checksummed. A field that could not be read is null rather than zero. Every response carries updatedAt as a millisecond timestamp.

Caching and stability

Responses are revalidated every 30 seconds. A stable API can add fields, but should not remove existing fields or change their meaning without a versioned path. Read defensively and never index a response by position.

The SDK and MCP

An SDK can expose market IDs, share and asset arithmetic, health factors, liquidation prices and transaction builders. MCP can let an agent read a position and build a transaction without driving a browser. Signing and submission still require the user's authorisation.

Fees and what they pay for

The reference describes one vault fee: 10% of the interest a vault earns. Nothing is charged on a deposit, withdrawal, borrow, repayment or liquidation, and there is no flash-loan fee in that model. If the vault earns nothing, no performance fee is earned.

The performance fee is 0% for the first 90 days of a vault. Of what is collected, the reference describes 75% going to stakers and 25% to the treasury for audits, oracle coverage, keeper gas and initial liquidity. Borrowers may receive a portion of interest back through a weekly epoch, and a builder that referred volume may receive a share of the fee.

Fees charged by the underlying lending protocol are separate. Verify current contract configuration rather than assuming historical values remain active.

Being first into a market

A new market is empty, and both sides may be waiting for the other. Three mechanisms can make waiting useful.

Collateral needs no liquidity

A collateral-supply operation does not need to draw from the lending pool, so a borrower can be positioned before there is anything to borrow, owe nothing while waiting and take collateral back when allowed. When a lender arrives, borrowing can be a single transaction.

Demand is visible

Earn can show the value of collateral posted by accounts carrying no debt — people who have already paid gas to stand in the market. That can be a more useful signal to a lender than TVL alone.

The exit is not locked

Earn should show what is withdrawable now against what is supplied, read from the vault. Utilisation can still hold a withdrawal, and rates can climb as the pool empties.

What breaks, and what happens then

Every system has failure modes. Documentation should list the condition that triggers them, what users can observe and the recovery path.

Keeper stops

Independent liquidation paths should remain possible if a protocol-run keeper is offline.

Oracle stops answering

Transactions that depend on a missing or stale price should fail safely.

Liquidity disappears

A position may be harder to exit and a vault withdrawal may be constrained by utilisation.

Indexer falls behind

Critical on-chain decisions should not rely only on an incomplete indexer result.

Security

In the reference architecture, deposits, collateral, debt and liquidations live in Morpho Blue. Periphery contracts are intended not to hold user funds between transactions, and every path should finish with balances swept out. That property must be verified against deployed code.

Administrative powers

Administrative powers may include choosing which markets a vault lends into, setting caps within deployed limits and publishing credit scores that change rebates. These powers should not be confused with the ability to move deposits or change immutable market parameters.

Review and known open issues

Review reports should list the money path, findings, fixes, refutations and open items. TWAP pools must have enough observation slots for the requested window; markets that do not meet this condition should be capped or protected until corrected.

Addresses

Contract addresses must be verified against official deployment records and the relevant chain explorer before use. Reference-project addresses are not copied here as if they were Credora deployments.

Deployment integrity. Renaming a website does not transfer ownership of contracts or make reference-project addresses belong to Credora. Add verified Credora contract addresses here once they are provided and checked.

Glossary

LLTV

Liquidation loan-to-value. The ratio of debt to collateral value above which anyone may liquidate a position. Fixed at market creation.

Health factor

Maximum borrow divided by what is owed. Above 1.0 a position is safer; at 1.0 it may be liquidatable.

Liquidation price

The collateral price at which the health factor reaches 1.0, expressed in the same units as the ticker.

Liquidation premium

What a liquidator keeps for doing the work. It may be larger on riskier markets to compensate for execution risk.

Utilisation

Borrowed divided by supplied. It helps set the rate and can limit immediate withdrawals.

Shares vs assets

Debt and deposits can be tracked in shares while the assets they represent grow with interest. Repaying by shares can be exact; repaying by amount is a guess about the block in which a transaction lands.

TWAP

Time-weighted average price. An average over a window that is harder to move inside one transaction than a spot price.

Observation cardinality

How many past price points a Uniswap pool stores. If it holds fewer seconds than a TWAP window requires, the oracle may stop answering.

Stale feed

A Chainlink answer older than the protocol is willing to trust. Weekend pauses in stock feeds can be normal, so freshness limits may differ by asset.

Flash loan

Borrowing and repaying inside one transaction. It can let liquidation happen without the liquidator owning all the required capital upfront.

FAQ

Who holds my collateral?

In the reference architecture, the underlying lending protocol holds deposits and collateral. Verify the actual contract used by any deployment before depositing.

What does the protocol earn?

The reference documentation describes a performance fee on vault interest, waived for the first 90 days. Confirm the current fee configuration on chain.

Can a market be changed?

Parameters such as LLTV, oracle and assets are fixed at creation in the described architecture. Different parameters require a new market rather than an edit.

What is not built yet?

Availability varies by deployment. Check each feature's status and deployed contracts rather than assuming every documented feature is live.

Where can I find parameters for a specific market?

Open that market's detail page and review its assets, oracle, LLTV, cap, liquidity and current status.

A curation layer for lending markets: isolated markets that let supported assets back a loan. Verify which protocol holds deposits and collateral before using any live deployment.
